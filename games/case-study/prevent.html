The starting point

A lecture.
With a difference.

I received my first spontaneous round of applause during a Prevent lecture. I’m sure it wasn’t just the delivery: we’d had fun playing and learning together.

To kick things off, I created a short game for students to play before we moved into the presentation, videos and discussion. It gave us a shared experience to talk about, rather than starting with a room full of passive listeners.

01 / A world they recognise

Familiar streets.
New conversations.

What started as an idea for a walk around Brighton, talking to non-player characters (NPCs), grew into a game taking in all the East Sussex College Group sites.

The Brighton setting shifted to the MET. Many of my students progress there, and I’d already built the maps, so it made sense to keep it in the world. Familiar places gave students a reason to explore and someone to talk to along the way.

02 / Built with student feedback

Their ideas.
Part of the game.

I had great fun playtesting with my students and adding Easter eggs, including famous faces to find and talk to in each town. The students brought ideas of their own.

One wanted to help people experiencing homelessness, so we added a voucher-giving system. Others suggested travelling between locations by train or bus, or finding bicycles and skateboards to get around town. Their feedback helped shape what the game could become.

03 / Explore, experiment, discuss

A little freedom.
A reason to care.

The game is designed as a roughly ten-minute introduction. Students can explore, meet characters and try things out before we bring those experiences into the lesson.

A map and walking directions help players find their way. The aim is to get everyone involved quickly, with enough freedom to be curious and enough direction to keep the session moving.

04 / From play to reflection

The game opens
the conversation.

After playing, we move into a presentation, helpful videos and a series of scenarios that students work through on their phones. The game starts the conversation; the follow-up gives us time to think, discuss and explain our choices.

I added a code system so students could go straight to the relevant scenarios without jumping ahead of the session. It keeps the experience easy to navigate while letting me guide the pace of the lesson.

05 / The tools behind the world

Build the tools.
Then the game.

Behind the scenes, I drew on APIs and data sources including the Post Office, OpenStreetMap, National Rail and Forest Research to help build the maps.

As with most games, I needed to build some of the tools before I could build the game itself. The tile-map editor is one example: a way to work on the places, layers and details that make the world playable. The companion tile designer supports the visual building blocks.
